Machine learning project · classification
Can demographics and personality traits predict cannabis use?
Feature engineering, clustering without labels, a train/test split, a classifier, and a look at how well the model works.

1 · The dataset, and why I chose it

I used the Drug Consumption (Quantified) dataset (from UCI Machine Learning Repository)

This dataset compares self-reported drug use with demographics and certain personality traits. The demographics chosen are age, gender, education, country, and ethnicity. The personality measurements include neuroticism, extraversion, openness to experience, agreeableness, conscientiousness, impulsivity, and sensation seeking. One important thing to note is that this data was taken before cannabis was made legal in parts of the United States.

Cannabis use is classified into seven different levels, from CL0 (Never Used) to CL6 (Used in Last Day).

Why it interests me: This dataset interests me becuase of the implications of the results. If drug use could be better predicted and understood, there could be better systems in place to help people struggling with addiction.

2 · Feature engineering

2.1 Class (im)balance

The class imbalance is mostly evenly distributed, with the exceptions of CL0 and CL6 being higher than the others. I will keep my train/test splits stratified so they will have equal distributions of cannabis use.

2.2 Feature histograms and outliers

All the data for the personality traits appears to be generally normally distributed. There are no outliers that need to be removed.

2.3 Missing values

There are no missing values in this dataset.

2.4 Encoding text fields

Most of the numeric data has already been normalized to work in a machine learning environment. However, I chose to one-hot encode the demographic features: age, gender, country, education, and ethnicity. This way, higher/lower numerical values that represent different categories will not affect the data. The reason why I chose not to ordinal encode Education is because there are too many distinct categories, and ranking them would not be accurate.

2.5 Correlated columns

The personality measures are most likely to be correlated because of their common normal distribution. The highest correlation is between SS and impulsiveness, but at a 0.62 value, it is not high enough to remove.

3 · Looking for clusters

There is no clear angle in the elbow graph, so I found the silhouette scores for X as well. The silhouette graph shows that the optimal number of clusters is 2.

This scatterplot is a result of running the K-Means Clustering algorithm on the X dataset. Before that, I applied Principal Component Analysis. PCA reduces the features to two dimensions so the clusters can be seen.

In order to interpret which features define the two clusters, we can look at the standardized feature means. Cluster 0 averages higher impulsivity and sensation seeking, with the opposite being true for cluster 1. The clusters are also separated largely by age and country. When the cannabis use feature is added back, these clusters represent respondents who answered C0 and C6.

4 · Train / test split

train: 1507 respondents    |   test: 377 respondents

The model is fitted on 80% of the data, and tested on 20%.

5 · Choosing an algorithm

In order to predict the discrete levels of cannabis use, I needed a classification algorithm. I chose the Random Forest algorithm. This algorithm creates multiple decision trees using different samples and features, then combines their predictions for a final answer.

6 · Training & testing the model

I trained a Random Forest classifier with 200 decision trees on the training set. The inputs were the demographics and personality of each respondent, and the target value was the cannabis use level.

I used the trained model to predict the use level for the remaining respondents. Because the final answers are discrete, the data can only be exactly right or wrong, but some inaccuracies are much more extreme than others.

7 · Confusion matrix / accuracy

The confusion matrix shows the correct predictions across the diagonal. On the 377 test respondents, the model had an overall accuracy of 41.9%. However, the accuracy was almost entirely from predicting the extreme values, C0 and C6.

When predicting C0 and C6, the model's accuracy is 75.7%. But when they are removed, the accuracy drops to only 10.7%.

8 · Findings about the data

Personality: Openness to experience was the most important decision factor, with the second and third being neuroticism and conscientiousness. All 7 traits are approximately within the same 8-12% range.

Demographics: Education, country, and age have nearly the same weight. However, the demographic features contributed less than half as much to the predictions as the personality features. Ethnicity and gender were both under 3% importance.

Between personality traits and demographics, the former is a much stronger feature in predicting cannabis use, at 74.2% vs 25.8%. However, none of the individual features have an importance of more than 12%. There is no one clear feature that leads to the prediction.

9 · About the model

Now we can look back at the two distinct clusters. The clusters represent C0 and C6 respectively. The model is at its best when predicting the extremes, and these clusters are grouped around them.

Prediction accuracy: The random forest was exactly right for 41.9% of its predictions, but the accuracy drops to just 10.9% when C0 and C6 are removed.

With an accuracy of 75.7% for C0 and C6, this model could be considered an accurate predictor for highs and lows. It works best predicting only the extreme values. It falls short predicting everything in-between.
